Research project

Characterizing circular jobs and their quality in Quebec’s social and circular economy

What kinds of jobs exist in Quebec’s social and circular economy, and what is their quality?

About

Growing interest in the circular economy (CE) as a response to sustainable development (SD) requirements raises many questions about the transformations it may generate, particularly in the labour market. While the literature broadly agrees that CE has the potential to create jobs on a net basis, much less is known about the kinds of jobs created and their quality. The intrinsic compatibility between the social and solidarity economy (SSE) and CE suggests that combining the two could support desirable, locally rooted employment. This research therefore examines jobs in Quebec’s social and circular economy. Which circularity strategies are associated with which types of jobs? Do the democratic governance and participation principles of the SSE contribute to job quality? Who appears to have access to these positions? Rather than claiming definitive answers, the project develops lines of inquiry and advances knowledge of this labour market.

Methods

The master’s thesis uses a mixed-methods design. Its main method is a Quebec-wide survey of social economy enterprises operating in the circular economy. Conducted in collaboration with TIESS (Territoires innovants en économie sociale et solidaire), the research first developed a directory of Quebec social and circular economy enterprises. A survey was then distributed to these organizations. In total, 219 respondents participated in the study of working and employment conditions. The data were analyzed using both quantitative and qualitative methods.

Results

Circular economy employment covers a wide variety of occupations and skill levels, and their distribution within firms varies according to the circularity strategies adopted. Upstream strategies focused on source reduction employ a larger share of qualified or highly qualified workers in management, coordination and design roles. Downstream strategies focused on recovery or life extension employ a larger share of workers with lower formal qualification requirements. The study also highlights the strong presence of work-integration social enterprises in these strategies, consistent with the historical relationship between the social economy and the circular economy.

Beyond this historical relationship, the social economy may act as a lever for circular job quality through its democratic and participatory governance principles and its social-purpose mission. Our findings show that employees’ satisfaction with their pay is positively associated with alignment between their own values and those of the organization. Flexible and adaptable schedules, which are common in the social economy, were also identified by participants as a significant benefit. We further highlight the importance of group information channels—team meetings, informal exchanges, platform messages and general assemblies—and frequent interactions among members of the organization, which appear to strengthen engagement and improve psychosocial working conditions.

Bringing the social and solidarity economy together with the circular economy therefore opens promising avenues for creating desirable jobs compatible with a just circular transition, particularly through organizational practices that encourage democratic and participatory governance.
